# The Buddha I Never Expected to Receive

*Sometimes, the most meaningful gifts come from the people we least expect.*

The Buddha I Never Expected to Receive
Sometimes, the most meaningful gifts come from the people we least expect.
For a few years, I have worked as a software engineer at Adobe. Like most people in technology, I have been fortunate to work on many different products and alongside people from many cultures. Every project taught me something new—not only about technology, but also about people.
One project, however, taught me a lesson I never expected.
Among my teammates was another Vietnamese engineer. He was considerably older than I was, and from the very beginning, we rarely agreed on anything. We challenged each other's ideas in meetings. Our discussions were often intense, and sometimes they became arguments. There were days when I walked out of a meeting thinking, "I don't want to debate with him ever again."
Then one afternoon, something unexpected happened.
As I walked past his desk, I noticed a small wooden Buddha statue.
It wasn't expensive or ornate. Yet there was something about it that immediately drew me in. The peaceful expression, the warmth of the carved wood, the quiet simplicity—it felt different from every Buddha statue I had seen before.
I couldn't stop thinking about it.
Over the following months, I searched everywhere.
Amazon.
eBay.
Vietnamese websites.
Small Buddhist stores.
I even searched while traveling.
But I never found another one like it.
Many times I wanted to ask him where he had bought it.
But every time I remembered our relationship, I stopped myself.
How could I ask someone I constantly disagreed with to sell me something he clearly cherished?
So I quietly gave up.
Some things, I told myself, simply aren't meant to be.
Time passed.
The project continued for more than a year.
Then one day, he announced that he was retiring.
For several days I wrestled with a question.
Should I ask?
Ninety-nine percent of me believed the answer would be "No."
Still, something inside me whispered,
"If you never ask, you'll never know."
So I gathered my courage.
I walked over to him and said,
"I know this may sound strange... but if you ever decide to let go of that Buddha statue, I would love to buy it from you."
He looked at me.
He smiled.
Then he said something I will never forget.
"You can have it."
I was stunned.
Before I could even ask how much, he added,
"No... it's a gift."
For a moment, I didn't know what to say.
This was the same person with whom I had disagreed countless times.
The same person I believed probably didn't like me very much.
Yet in that moment, none of our disagreements seemed to matter anymore.
As I carried the statue home that day, I realized something profound.
Perhaps we spend too much of our lives assuming we know what is in another person's heart.
Perhaps conflict is only one small part of a much larger story.
Perhaps people become gentler when they no longer feel the need to protect their pride, defend their opinions, or hold tightly to what they own.
Whenever I look at this Buddha statue today, I no longer remember our arguments.
I remember kindness.
I remember generosity.
And I remember that the most valuable things we receive in life are rarely the things we buy.
They are the things that arrive through conditions we never could have planned.
Perhaps this is what Buddhism calls Duyên—not destiny, not coincidence, but the quiet meeting of countless conditions, unfolding at exactly the right moment.
