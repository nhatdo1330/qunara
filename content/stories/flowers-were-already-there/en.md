# The Flowers Were Already There

> *What changed was not the road I walked, but the mind that was walking through it.*

## The Walk I Took Every Day

I work at Adobe, and whenever my schedule allows, I leave my desk during lunch and take a short walk. It is not a long route—just a familiar street near the office lined with flowering plants. The walk gives my eyes a break from screens and my body a chance to move before the afternoon begins.

For weeks, the plants along that street had been covered with bright yellow blossoms.

I know this because I saw them later.

At the time, however, I barely noticed them.

I walked the same sidewalk, passed the same trees, crossed the same intersections, and returned to the office every afternoon. My feet followed the path almost automatically while my mind stayed somewhere else.

It stayed with unfinished work.

With meetings I had not yet attended.

With problems that still needed solutions.

With messages waiting for replies.

With the quiet pressure that accumulates without announcing itself.

From the outside, I probably looked relaxed. I was outdoors, walking in the California sunshine.

Inside, I was still sitting at my desk.

Looking back, I realize that I was present only in the physical sense. My attention had never really left work.

## The Day I Finally Saw the Yellow

One afternoon, something was different.

Not the weather.

Not the street.

Not the flowers.

Earlier that day I had mentally let go of some of what had been weighing on me. The work itself had not disappeared, but I was no longer carrying every unfinished task in my head at once. My thoughts felt quieter, less crowded.

As I walked the same familiar route, something caught my eye.

The flowers.

Their yellow seemed almost startling. They stood out against the green leaves with a brightness that made me slow down. For the first time in days, I actually looked at them instead of walking past them.

I stopped for a moment.

The flowers had not suddenly appeared.

They had already been there.

The change had happened somewhere else.

It had happened in me.

That realization stayed with me for the rest of the day.

Nothing remarkable occurred afterward. There was no dramatic turning point. But conversations seemed easier. Small inconveniences felt less irritating. I noticed more smiles. I felt more patient. By evening, I realized I had experienced an ordinary day in a way that felt unexpectedly light.

The environment had remained almost exactly the same.

My experience of it had not.

## Was It a Lucky Day?

That afternoon made me think about something people often describe as having "positive energy."

It is an expression I have heard all my life, and I still use it occasionally in everyday conversation.

But what do I actually mean by it?

I do not mean an invisible force that attracts good fortune.

I do not mean that thoughts somehow reshape the physical world through mysterious laws.

What I mean is much simpler.

There are mornings when I wake up feeling rested, calm, and mentally unburdened. Before looking at my phone, before reading the news, before stepping into work, there is a quietness that accompanies the beginning of the day.

On mornings like those, the day often feels different.

Not because traffic disappears.

Not because meetings become shorter.

Not because every problem solves itself.

Instead, I seem to meet those same circumstances differently.

I notice people more.

I listen more carefully.

I become a little more patient when something unexpected happens.

I recognize opportunities that I might otherwise overlook.

The day itself may not have changed nearly as much as my way of participating in it.

That raises an interesting question.

Did "positive energy" bring good things into my day?

Or did a lighter state of mind allow me to notice—and take part in—more of the good that was already there?

I am not certain the distinction can always be measured.

But I think it is worth considering.

## What We Carry Changes What We Notice

Attention is never neutral.

When my mind is crowded with deadlines and worries, it narrows. It searches for problems because problems feel urgent. Everything else fades quietly into the background.

Beauty does not disappear.

Gratitude does not disappear.

Kindness does not disappear.

They simply become easier to miss.

When the mental load becomes lighter, attention seems to widen again.

The same street contains more color.

The same conversation contains more warmth.

The same afternoon contains more moments that feel alive.

Nothing magical has happened.

The world has not transformed overnight.

But the part of the world I am able to notice has become larger.

I wonder how often this happens without my realizing it.

How many ordinary moments have I walked past while thinking only about the next task?

How many flowers have I failed to see?

## What I Mean by “Positive Energy”

When I say someone has positive energy, I am usually describing a way of being rather than a mysterious force.

Someone who is calm.

Open.

Hopeful.

Attentive.

Less burdened by constant mental noise.

Ready to meet other people with curiosity instead of defensiveness.

That state often changes how we respond.

When we listen more carefully, conversations become richer.

When we smile more naturally, people often respond in kind.

When we pay attention, opportunities that were always present become easier to recognize.

None of this requires believing in an invisible human energy field.

It simply acknowledges that our inner condition influences our perception, our behavior, and the countless small interactions that shape an ordinary day.

## A Buddhist-Inspired Reflection

One idea from Buddhist practice has become increasingly meaningful to me over the years.

Mindfulness is not primarily about creating a different world.

It is about learning to be present within the one that already exists.

When the mind is restless, reality becomes filtered through worry, desire, or distraction.

When the mind becomes quieter, the same reality often appears differently.

The flowers along my lunchtime walk did not become more beautiful on the day I noticed them.

They had been beautiful all along.

My attention had finally become available.

I do not take this as proof of any spiritual doctrine.

It is simply a small experience that helps me understand why mindfulness places so much importance on attention.

Before changing the world we experience, we may first need to notice the world we already inhabit.

## What Science Can — and Cannot — Say

Modern psychology offers several ideas that fit comfortably with this experience.

Under stress, attention often narrows toward potential problems. Researchers describe forms of selective attention and negativity bias that help explain why our minds can become preoccupied with threats while overlooking neutral or pleasant details. A more positive emotional state may broaden attention, allowing us to notice a wider range of information. Mindfulness practices also appear to strengthen present-moment awareness rather than leaving attention trapped in habitual patterns.

These ideas help explain why the same street might seem different from one day to the next.

They do not demonstrate that human thoughts emit a measurable field that attracts fortunate events.

Science can say quite a bit about attention, perception, emotion, and behavior.

It says far less about claims that extend beyond observable evidence.

For me, that boundary is worth respecting.

The story of the flowers does not need anything more extraordinary than an ordinary human mind learning, however briefly, to pay attention.

## Ten Quiet Minutes Before the Day Begins

If this story resonates with you, I would like to suggest a small experiment.

Tomorrow morning, before checking your phone, spend ten or fifteen quiet minutes without work, email, news, or social media.

Look out a window or step outside.

Notice the quality of the light.

Notice the sky.

Listen for a single sound you might normally ignore.

Find one living thing—a bird, a tree, a flower, even a patch of grass—and give it your full attention for a moment.

Think of one thing you are grateful for.

Then consider one small good thing you might bring into someone else's day.

Do not expect the day to become luckier.

Do not expect every problem to disappear.

Simply observe whether you experience the day differently.

## A Question to Carry With You

I still walk that same street near the office.

The flowers bloom, fade, and bloom again with the seasons.

Whenever I notice them now, I remember that afternoon when I suddenly realized they had never been missing.

Only my attention had been.

The flowers did not need to bloom again for me.

I needed to become available to see them.

*What might you notice tomorrow if you gave your mind ten quiet minutes before giving it to the world?*
